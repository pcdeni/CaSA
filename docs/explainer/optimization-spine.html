1 · The three places

Everything in this document happens in one of three places. Keeping them straight is the whole game, because the fast operations and the slow operations live in different places, and the cost of a design is mostly the cost of moving between them.

The asymmetry that shapes every decision: compute inside DRAM costs nanoseconds; the host↔FPGA hop over PCIe costs about 1.5 ms — a million times more. We are host-orchestrated: the CPU drives each step, so the cost of a design is dominated by how many of those hops it takes.

Everything else — attention scores, softmax, normalization — is rounding error in the compute budget for single-token decode. So "run an LLM in memory" reduces to "do a matvec in memory, 210 times per token."

The seven per-layer matvecs are the query/key/value/output projections and the FFN gate/up/down projections. A matvec is y_m = Σ_j W_{m,j} · x_j: each output element y_m is a dot product of one weight-matrix row with the activation vector x. For BitNet the weights W are ternary — every entry is −1, 0, or +1. That ternary structure is what makes an in-DRAM implementation possible at all, and it is where §4 begins.

3 · Three DRAM primitives (defined before we use them)

A DRAM array is rows of capacitor cells sharing bitlines and sense amplifiers. Activating a row copies its charge onto the bitlines. If you break the timing rules — activate in ways the standard forbids — the analog behavior of the bitlines gives you three operations for free, without moving data off the chip.

Primitive A — Row copy (RowClone)

Activate a source row, then a destination row before the bitlines settle. The destination is overwritten with the source's bits. One row is duplicated into another, entirely inside the array. We use it to place operands where a compute step needs them.

Primitive B — MAJ3, the in-DRAM AND

Activate three rows at once (again, mistimed). Each bitline resolves to the majority of the three cells: MAJ3(A,B,C) is 1 where at least two of A,B,C are 1. Now tie the third row to all-zeros: the majority of {A, B, 0} is 1 exactly when both A and B are 1. So

MAJ3(A, B, 0) = A AND B

That bitwise AND, done by the sense amplifiers on 2048 bits at once, is the multiply step of the whole system. It happens in DRAM and costs a handful of DDR commands.

Primitive C — popcount

After the AND, we need to know how many bit positions came out 1 — that count is the partial dot product. Counting the ones in a row is popcount. It can run on the host (read the row back, count in the CPU) or in the FPGA fabric (count on the card, return only the total). Where popcount happens is one of the two walls in §5.

A fourth, harder primitive — MAJ5. Majority of five rows is the sum bit of a full adder (carry is a MAJ3), so a MAJ5/MAJ3 pair adds numbers inside the array. This is how you accumulate without reading anything out — the basis of "in-DRAM accumulation" (Road-B, §6, and MVDRAM's adder, §10). On our SK-hynix dies MAJ5 is measurably harder than MAJ3: at 16 co-activated rows MAJ3 yields hundreds of whole-row-perfect tuples while MAJ5 yields zero (its per-cell stability tops out at 99.98–99.99%, a thin replication margin), so faithful MAJ5 needs per-column screening to become bit-exact. MAJ3 is the workhorse; MAJ5 is the frontier.

4 · One matvec, in memory

Take a single output element of the query projection, y_m = Σ_{j=0}^{2559} W_{m,j} · x_j. Two facts make it fit the primitives above.

(1) Split the ternary weight into two 1-bit masks. Let W₊ mark the +1 positions and W₋ mark the −1 positions. Then Σ_j W_{m,j} x_j = Σ_{W=+1} x_j − Σ_{W=−1} x_j.

(2) Split the activation into bitplanes. Write each integer x_j = Σ_b 2^b · x_j^{(b)} with each x_j^{(b)} ∈ {0,1}. A sum of selected x_j becomes a place-value-weighted sum of bit counts. Putting both together:

y_m = Σb 2b · [ popcount( W₊m AND x(b) ) − popcount( W₋m AND x(b) ) ]
each AND = one MAJ3 in DRAM · each popcount = count the 1s · then scale by 2ᵇ and subtract

Every term is now a primitive from §3. Here is the same computation as work spread across the three places — this is the "what happens where and when" picture:

The compute is cheap; the trips are not. The MAJ3 and its result live in DRAM; the FPGA issues the commands. But the two red arrows — writing operands in, reading the row back out — are PCIe round-trips at ~1.5 ms each, and a full token does thousands of them. That is the naive baseline. Every optimization in §6 attacks one of those two arrows.

The 1-bit case is cheaper still. Bonsai-1bit has weights in {−1,+1} (no zero). With only two values, Σ_j W_j x_j = 2·Σ_{W=+1} x_j − Σ_j x_j: you compute one mask's popcount and recover the rest arithmetically, because Σ_j x_j is a constant you already have. This is the single-track protocol (internally V2S): one mask instead of two halves the operand data crossing PCIe. It is why 1-bit ends up faster than ternary despite being "more quantized" (§7).

5 · The two walls

The naive matvec above is correct but slow, and it is slow for two specific, measured reasons.

Wall 1 — Readout

To get one popcount, the naive path reads a whole result row back to the host. Across a real model that is about 16 bits moved per weight versus the 2 bits a weight actually is — an 8.0× readout amplification (new/exp0_readout_floor.py). At DDR4-2400 the readout alone is ~217 ms against a same-channel CPU's ~27–29 ms: 7.5× slower on readout before any compute. A host-accumulation shape cannot beat the CPU — which is exactly why counting and summing have to move onto the card or into the array (Road-B, in-DRAM adders).

Wall 2 — Round-trip

Each program is a host→FPGA→host hop over PCIe, and that hop is latency-bound at ~1.5 ms (interrupt-wake dominated; a bigger read barely costs more, so "fewer, larger reads" is the lever, not "less data"). With thousands of programs per token, the trips dominate. Measured request profile (srv-prof, production shape):

recv is 65% of the wall — the latency of getting results back, trip-count-bound not byte-bound (a bigger read barely costs more). exec at 19% includes the round-trip and so is mostly trip time too, not DRAM compute. Note the consequence: the actual in-DRAM arithmetic is a sliver. An optimization only matters if it shrinks recv or removes trips — improving the compute the array already does for free buys nothing.
This split is the ranking function for every lever. A change that cuts wcol from 8.6% to zero is capped at an 8.6% win no matter how impressive its internal ratio looks. A change that halves recv is worth 32%. Several plausible-sounding optimizations died on exactly this arithmetic (§8), and one — M3 — looked like a 265× win internally while attacking the 8.6% slice (§6, §8).
Two costs, not one. The 65% you feel is latency (trip count) — attacked today by orchestration, and in silicon by an on-die sequencer that ends the round-trip (first evidence is now on the bench: a Rung-2a orchestrator probe returns 27/27 byte-exact integer partials against a CPU oracle in Verilator, with build-27 in flight). The 8× readout amplification is bandwidth (bytes) — real, but hidden behind latency until the trips are cut, then attacked by in-die popcount / MAJ5. Neither row runs unless the sense amp permits multi-row activation — the SA-vs-OCSA question that decides whether this is mass-producible at all.

6 · The optimization ladder

Each lever is grouped by which wall it attacks, with what it exploits, what it costs, and its evidence class. This is the spine: the naive matvec of §4 climbs from correct-but-slow toward the CPU by removing trips and readout, never by making the free part freer.

Why M3 is on the ladder at all. It is the cleanest illustration of the §5 ranking rule. Its internal ratio (265×) is real in isolation, but in the live dataflow it targets the smallest slice and its mechanism was never actually wired to the row that gets read. Both facts had to be measured to kill it — an impressive internal number is not a wall win, and a passing token gate is not a numerics proof (the layer-0 gate passed with 87% of masks stale; a max-tokens-1 gate later flattered X-master the same way). The adoption bar we settled on is per-op correlation (corr ≥ 0.98) at production depth — never token identity or a shallow shape — and that instrument is what killed both M3 and X-master. It stays here as the worked example of that lesson.

The second campaign — activation quant + phase-2 (the current production stack)

Two levers landed after the ladder above and now define the frontier. Activation quant (PIM_ACT_K) sends fewer bitplanes per activation — K=6 instead of 8 — which is fewer trips per matvec; it is client-side, no bitstream, and token-identical (gated on per-op correlation, not token identity — the §6 lesson). Phase-2 send-ahead (§6) is the first lever to cut recv. Bisected on the BitNet dimm2 full-model path ("capital of France?" → "…Paris" at every rung):

This is the current frontier number, and it supersedes the never-materialized "~2×" that X-master was once projected to reach.

A second, orthogonal quant axis — the activation. On top of the weight quant above, each family ships a coarser activation-plane default (PIM_ACT_K) that sends fewer bitplanes per activation — client-side, no bitstream, correlation-gated token-identical: K=6 for both Bonsai variants (1-bit −21.7%, ternary −22.2% full-model wall) and K=5 for BitNet-2B (−32.2%). The floor tracks the training recipe, not the weight bits — K=4 collapses everywhere (a genuine quant cliff), which is why the default differs per family.

What MVDRAM evaluates: four models end to end — Llama2-7B, Llama2-13B, Llama3-8B, Phi-4 — at 2-bit and 4-bit, on llama.cpp with the matmul replaced (256 tokens × 10 runs), plus GeMV kernel benchmarks (e.g. 32000×4096). Two facts to state plainly: the paper releases no source code, and it carries no accuracy or perplexity evaluation — reported correctness rests entirely on screening reliable DRAM columns. What we reproduced: their mechanisms bit-exact, from the paper alone, and the GeMV validated to exact FP32 against a CPU reference on a real Llama-2-7B tensor — not the full four-model throughput benchmark (multi-day per model on one rig; we ran a sampled end-to-end instead).

10 · MVDRAM — the peer system, and where we can go past it

MVDRAM (arXiv 2503.23817, Kubo et al., UTokyo + MSR) is the closest published system: low-bit LLM GeMV in unmodified DDR4 on DRAM Bender, which the paper reports as faster than the CPU end-to-end (no source released, and no accuracy or perplexity evaluation — see §9). It uses the same primitives, composed differently. Three mechanisms, as the paper describes them:

  • On-the-fly vector encoding. A partial product is a selective RowClone: for activation bit a, copy the weight row iff a=1 (else copy a zero row, or skip on sparsity). Weights are only ever sources, so they are restored for free and never reloaded.
  • Horizontal matrix layout. The matrix stays row-major; many output columns compute in parallel across ~60k columns; outputs read out row-wise with no bit-transposition.
  • In-DRAM accumulation. MAJ-based full adders (carry = MAJ3, sum = MAJ5) sum inside the array; only the q×r output rows are ever read back.

Improving on it, beyond reproduction

  • Adopt selective-RowClone products for ternary. Their q=2 case is exactly our pos/neg masks, and it removes the per-MAJ3 weight reload that dominates our cost. Now validated on our silicon: LANE2_ENCODE=clone gives 3.2× end-to-end at 4096² with 5× fewer per-column writes, weights resident / restored-free / never reloaded — their core sidestep of our per-MAJ3 reload cost. The remaining piece is reduction (we cannot run their MAJ5 tree natively) — a per-column FPGA accumulator in the readback path fills it.
  • Our column-screening rescues their MAJ5. On our dies MAJ5 is 99.98% stable — only ~0.4–2 flaky columns per 2048. Bit-exact MAJ5 accumulation does exist here (524,288/524,288 partials exact on real blk.0.attn_q) — but a static clean-column permutation alone is not the closer: the residual is transient-dominated (chained screening bought only +0.4%), so the working route is screening plus temporal VOTE3, not permutation by itself.
  • Their error model is blind to our XOR-spread. MVDRAM screens columns statically; it cannot see the row-geometry-dependent deterministic corruption we characterized (a multi-row activation deposits one operand's bits into predictable neighbor rows). On chips with that fingerprint, their millions of RowClones with weight rows as sources would corrupt in patterns per-column profiling cannot predict. That characterization is a genuine addition, not a reproduction.

11 · Where the work actually stands (2026-07-28)

Stated honestly, by evidence class, so the picture is not rosier than the measurements:

  • ✓ production, measured — phase-2 send-ahead (−26.3%, recv 112 → 57 ms), now the production default; K-quant across all three families (−21.7% / −22.2% / −32.2%, token-identical); V2_PACK (−12.7%), SEG_POP (byte collapse), dual-DIMM (1.95×), 1-bit single-track (18.7 s/tok), Road-B in-fabric accumulation (213× readout cut). Fused-coset (1.63×) is a real win but opt-in (default OFF). The current production stack is K6 + fused + phase-2 = −64.7% vs the K=8 baseline (973.2 → 343.7 s, "…Paris"), and the Bonsai weight-quant ladder is 5.36× stacked, all token-identical.
  • ⚠ measured wall-neutral (alone) — Rung-1 streaming produces correct tokens but does not move the wall by itself: recv is synchronous, so streaming the send cannot overlap it. It is the enabler, not the win — the win is phase-2 (above), which pipelines sends past recvs and did land.
  • ✗ dead — M3 coset-broadcast in the server (excised, retracted), X-master-clone (charge-shared seed, closed negative, V2S hard-gated), K-batching, word-grain sparsity skip, threshold-sweep reduction. Recorded so they are not re-attempted.
  • ▢ designed / first evidence — the on-die orchestrator (Rung-2a) that would end the round-trip entirely: a probe already returns 27/27 byte-exact integer partials against a CPU oracle in Verilator (07-27), with build-27 (magic 0xDBC0DE16) in flight to the fabric.
The honest headline. recv — the latency of the round-trips that carry results back, not their byte volume — was ~65% of the wall, and for months every production win only trimmed the other 35%. That story is now over: phase-2 send-ahead is the first production win to cut recv itself (halved, and now the default), K-quant took another axis, and the two compose into the −64.7% stack. The compute inside DRAM was never the problem, and making it freer never was the answer. What's next is the round-trip count, not the byte volume: the Rung-2a on-die orchestrator (build-27) to collapse each projection to a single trip; the reopened faithful-MAJ5 line (bit-exact via screening + temporal VOTE3, §8/§10); and the phase-B / phase-P consolidation — benchmarking and publishing the stack that now stands.
