One command pair, two physics

A single pair of memory commands — open a row, close the array, open a second row — is one primitive with one timing dial, and it opens a fixed, predictable family of rows: the two rows it names plus their relatives at fixed address offsets (here 4, 16, 64 and 512), a family wired into the chip's row decoder and identical every time. At copy timing the first row's content spreads across that whole family at once — a real, measured multi-row copy (DRAM's own RowClone, run here on purpose), and the reason the weights are laid out to keep operands off one another's families. At vote timing nothing is imported: the family's rows are counted, and their bit-by-bit majority wins. One primitive, one dial — copy spreads, vote counts. This page shows where the line between them sits, why the family is exactly the rows it is, and how, when a copy lands on a vote's operands, a wrong answer becomes something you can predict down to the byte.

New here? This page is the physics. For the plain-language tour of how a whole language model runs inside a DRAM chip — cells, rows, the write → compute → read loop — start with the system explainer, then come back.

1A row address is just bits

Every row in a DRAM chip has a numeric address. To open a row, the chip feeds that address into a decoder — a small tree of logic that lights up one wordline, the wire connecting a row of storage cells to the chip's sense circuitry. The decoder does not treat the address as one number. It splits it into groups of bits and hands each group to a small predecoder. Hold that picture: the grouping decides everything later on this page.

Because addresses are bits, the natural way to relate two rows is by which bit differs. Here, row addresses are written in decimal, and a relationship between two rows is named by the separating bit: two rows "differ in bit 4" when flipping bit 4 of one address gives the other. Each bit is worth a fixed amount — the four this page uses:

2The double activation — one command pair

The whole story is a single sequence of four commands to the memory:

ACT (row A)  → first hold →  PRE  → gap →  ACT (row B)

ACT opens a row; PRE ("precharge") closes the array again. Two timing knobs shape what happens:

  • the first hold — how many command slots the first row stays open before the array is closed (t12), and
  • the gap — how many slots the array precharges before the second row opens (t23).

Shorten these and the array never fully settles between the two activations: the second ACT can open its row while the first is still driving the shared wires. That overlap is the source of both behaviours below. It is not a defect — it is exactly the mechanism SiMRA's Multi-RowCopy uses on purpose (credited in full at the end).

Read it off the dial. At the operating point (0–1 slots) the pair resolves a majority vote — every participating row ends holding the winning value. One extra slot of hold and the sense amplifiers land undecided: a tie. Three or more slots and the pair has become a copy — the first-opened row (the source) is written into the others. Same four commands; the timing alone picks the physics.

This boundary holds on all four dies we own — two SK hynix and two Micron. On every one, a well-timed operation never imports a foreign value: the copy regime sits a few slots away, and where it begins is a per-die number (SK hynix here: copy at 3 slots; the Micron dies' copy regime begins a few slots later, around slot 5).

But "clean" has an honest scope, and a model-free census — unique fingerprints on every row of a ~640-row window, hundreds of random uncalibrated pairs, no assumptions about where effects may land — pins it down: on the SK hynix dies, a vote-timing operation always co-activates the pair's whole coset — the rows the selection law names — and resolves them together, to one shared value. Zero of 200 random pairs leave the rows untouched; 70–73% recruit rows beyond the two named. A vote is "clean" when the co-activated rows already agree — which is exactly what calibration and operand replication arrange, so a calibrated majority comes back looking untouched. Give the co-activated rows disagreeing data and the same operation rewrites them all with the joint result: one uncalibrated pair here leaves its entire 8-row coset holding one shared value — the equal-weight per-bit majority of everything the eight rows hold, exact on every clear-majority bit, with exact ties falling to a fixed per-region preference. The same pair at copy timing deposits byte-exactly into the same 8 rows. Same coset, same law; the timing picks vote-together versus copy-from-source, and both outcomes are deterministic.

Two further edges. At an exact per-bit tie — half the co-activated rows say 1, half say 0 — the sense amplifiers fall back to a fixed preference that varies by row region and does not follow anyone's data (flip the first row's content and the tie resolves the same way). An instrument that manufactures ties — for example filler rows the recipe assumes are charge-weakened but the silicon leaves at full strength — will misread that fixed preference as instability: on one of our Micron dies this drops one tuple's characterization score to ~3% at the clean operating point while a sibling tuple in a region of opposite tie preference scores ~79%. And the co-activation mechanism itself is die-family-scoped: the Micron dies do not follow the hynix selection law and often fail to co-activate at vote timing at all — a different decoder regime, not a cleaner vote.

A second knob: the gap to the second activation

The other knob — the gap after PRE, before the second ACT — controls a different line: whether the pair copies into two rows or into many. A clean two-row copy needs the array fully closed, which takes at least 4 slots (6.0 ns). Below that, the copy spreads across a whole set of neighbours.

First-hit-wins. The standard SiMRA calibration scans this gap upward from 1 slot and stops at the first value that clones successfully — which is 1 slot, inside multi-row territory. So a calibrated "RowClone" is already a multi-row operation: it copies into the neighbour set too. That is not a reliability choice; it is simply where a first-hit search happens to stop. Production keeps this short timing on purpose (see §7).

4The vote, derived

"Majority vote" means exactly what it says: for each bit position, the result takes whichever value appears in more of the rows. Here are three one-byte values and the majority they produce — the only place on the page where bits are shown one at a time. Everywhere else, bytes are written in hex.

 A   1 1 1 1 0 0 0 0   F0
 B   1 1 0 0 1 1 0 0   CC
 C   1 0 1 0 1 0 1 0   AA
     ───────────────
MAJ  1 1 1 0 1 0 0 0   E8

Each column of the bottom row is the majority of the three above it: MAJ(F0, CC, AA) = E8. In the demo the operand rows hold F0 (call it A), CC (B) or AA (C); a well-timed vote turns all of them into E8.

5Which rows the pair touches — the coset and the selection law

When the pair copies, it does not copy into one row. It copies into every row reachable from the source by adding any combination of the generators the two opened rows differ in. With the four generators from §1, that is a set of 16 rows — call it the coset — the source plus every subset-sum of {4, 16, 64, 512} on the base row 38408:

Which of the 16 actually fire — the selection law

Not every row in the coset always receives the copy; the predecoder grouping from §1 decides. On our SK hynix dies the subarray-local address bits fall into fixed groups — {1,2} {3,4} {5,6} {7,8}, with bits 0 and 9 on their own:

{1, 2} {3, 4} {5, 6} {7, 8} {0} {9}

A candidate row fires only when, inside each group, it takes either none or all of the generator bits in that group. In the example each of the four generators (bits 2, 4, 6, 9) sits alone in a different group, so each can be toggled independently — and all 16 combinations fire. When two generators share one group, they lock together, and fewer than the full set fire.

Digital selection, analog firing. Which rows are candidates is a property of the decoder wiring: bank-invariant, byte-exact, and identical across power cycles and rigs. On our two SK hynix dies this rule accounts for 1691 of 1691 observed rows with zero exceptions. Whether a candidate actually receives the copy is the analog part — set by the timing and charge of §3. A different manufacturer's die (Micron) shows the same physics with a different grouping.

6Good case and bad case, row by row

Now the payoff. Take the 16-row coset, load it with A, B and C so the honest majority is E8, and fire one well-timed vote. Layout: A on 6 rows (including both opened rows), B on 6, C on 4 — the worst tally at any bit is 6-against-10, so the honest answer wins by at least four votes and no single row can change it.

Bad case — a copy-timing deposit, then the same vote

Change one thing. Before the vote, use the copy regime to place the source F0 onto its four single-generator neighbours — the four rows from §5 holding C. This is a targeted copy, not the whole-coset flood the dial shows at deep copy timing: each neighbour is filled by its own small source-to-neighbour copy, so exactly those four flip and the other eleven rows stay put. Ten rows now hold F0 and six hold CC, so the vote returns F0 — not the honest E8 — and, being destructive, writes that F0 back into all 16 rows. The machine is not glitching; it is faithfully voting, over an operand set quietly rewritten first. And because it is a vote, the wrong answer arrives exactly when the count says it should:

A blanket copy would ignore k; a random glitch would not track a vote count. The flip at k = 3 is the signature of a genuine majority computed over substituted operands.

One honesty note. A raw vote is analog — repeated in the same session it agrees to within a fraction of a percent of the cells. The copy deposits, by contrast, are exact: every one of the 8 192 bytes matches the source. The tables show the logical result.

7Hazard and asset — decided by placement, not timing

The bad case looks like a trap, and left unmanaged it is one. But the very same coupling is a free one-to-many copy: fire the pair once and the source lands in its whole coset at no extra cost. Whether that is a hazard or a gift is decided by where you put your data, not by the timing.

  • Neutralize it by placement. Lay the weights out so no operand ever sits on a row the source would deposit into — choose addresses whose generators avoid the coset — and the deposit can never corrupt a vote. It is corruption-free by construction, with no reliance on tuning the timing. (The production layout technique and its numbers are in the peer doc.)
  • Or exploit it as broadcast. Put the value you want replicated on the source row and the coset fills itself. Production uses this: the fused-coset activation path replaces a per-row rewrite with one write plus one broadcasting pair, and runs today — measured 1.63×/token on our production server (a measured example). Broadcasting a weight into its whole coset is validated and queued.

This is why the short "dirty" calibration timing from §3 is kept on purpose. Widening the gap would suppress the extra deposits — but those extra deposits are free copies. Keep the short timing; make the spread harmless by placement. For the production throughput numbers and the wall model behind them, see the peer doc.

8Provenance and credit

The multi-row co-activation itself — one command pair opening a lattice of rows and driving them to the source's value — is SiMRA's own Multi-RowCopy, and the credit is theirs (DRAM-Bender #12). What this page adds is the address algebra — that the touched rows are exactly the coset of the pair's generators — the selection law for which members fire, and the consequence that a majority vote can run, silently, over a substituted operand set.

The candidate selection is byte-identical across our two SK hynix modules (two different part numbers), and a Micron die reproduces the same physics with a different grouping. Our exchange with the SiMRA authors is at DRAM-Bender #12 and SiMRA-DRAM #1.
