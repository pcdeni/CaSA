The experiment trail

What we tried, how it went (and why), and what each result pointed to next.

How to read this. Every entry has the same four parts: Tried (the idea), Result (what the silicon said), Why (the mechanism behind the outcome), and Pointed to (what it changed about the plan). The honest verdicts are the point — the wall-neutral and negative results built the cost model that now drives everything, and several of the biggest wins started life as failures.

win wall-neutral negative reversed later discovery lesson

Hardware shorthand: the rig drives four DDR4 modules through one FPGA (BCU1525). Benders 0 and 2 are two different SK hynix modules (bender 2 is the dual-rank production die); benders 1 and 3 are two different Micron modules. A "tuple" is a calibrated 16-row set that computes MAJ3; a "doubleACT" is the timing-violating double activation that makes rows share charge.

  • addendum n — numbered addenda of the 2026-07-17 session writeup (sublattice_broadcast_2026_07_17/RESULT.md).

Act I April – May · foundations

Characterize all four modules in parallel win

Tried Multi-DIMM plumbing in SiMRA so all four modules sweep at once.
Result The yield map: bender 2 best (~38% MAJ3 tuple yield), bender 0 usable, benders 1/3 partial.
Pointed to Bender 2 as the production die, bender 0 as the second compute die.

codes: multi-DIMM plumbing · CHECKPOINT-MULTI-DIMM.md · yield map (memory dimm_yield_map)

The XOR-8 "corruption" discovery

Tried Ordinary calibrated doubleACT operations.
Result Every operation also wrote the source row's bits into the row at address src XOR 8. It looked like data corruption.
Why Rows whose addresses differ in specific bits co-activate — the row decoder fires several wordlines under the violated timing.
Pointed to First a workaround (stride-16 pool layouts). Months later, the entire lattice program: the bug became the instrument.

codes: memory simra_xor8_spread · ancestor of task #80 → the M3 line

The readout floor negative

Tried A CaSA-shaped compute pipeline, benchmarked against a CPU.
Result Disqualified: reading the amplified outputs cost ~8× the useful data.
Why The shape produces more readout bytes than compute value; the host pays for every byte.
Pointed to "Readout is the enemy" became design law, and MVDRAM (arXiv 2503.23817) became the peer system to reproduce and compare against.

codes: exp0 (new/exp0_readout_floor.py) · new/MEASURED-RESULTS.md

BitNet, phase by phase win

Tried Bit-exact MAJ3 → batched matvec → real BitNet weights → all seven layer-0 projections → the model in the loop.
Result "What is the capital of France?" → "Paris", computed through DRAM charge-sharing (projections at 99.98%).
Pointed to Correctness was solved; the campaign became throughput.

codes: phases 0–4f · Lane 1 · memory bitnet_phase4f_pim_in_loop

The May bug chain lesson

Tried Chasing "degrading" and "flaky" full-model output.
Result Four separate deterministic software bugs (a pointer shifted 4 bytes by an in-band field, a divide where the model multiplies, a pool stride that placed twin rows, a stale cache key).
Why Byte-identical wrong output is logic, never noise.
Pointed to The house rule: structural before stochastic — "silicon lottery" is a scapegoat until an experiment proves randomness.

Act II June · the reproduction and the wall

MVDRAM reproduction reversed later

Tried Reproduce MVDRAM faithfully (their models, their conventions, kept in its own lane).
Result Correctness-faithful: achieved. Performance-faithful: blocked — their fast dataflow measured 6.1% accuracy here and was ruled "impossible" on our substrate.
Why (As discovered in July:) the placement was tripping co-activation; the verdict was a wrong model of the physics, not physics.
Pointed to A standing question the lattice work later answered — and a reminder that "impossible" verdicts need mechanisms attached.

codes: Lane 2 · ADR-001..004 · GAP-1/GAP-3 · Result A/B · mvdram-repro/

Tried Deciding between faithful in-DRAM readout (Road A) and accumulate-in-DRAM (Road B).
Result Keep both: Road A for fidelity claims, Road B to attack the readout wall.
Pointed to The eventual merge: BitNet with in-DRAM accumulation.

Act III July 17 · the lattice week

Sub-lattice broadcast win

Tried doubleACT between two members of a calibrated tuple at k-generator distance.
Result The source's data lands in exactly the predicted 2k sub-coset — bit-exact, zero leak, on both hynix dies.
Why Row-address bits partition into decoder groups; the pair distance selects which groups fire.
Pointed to The spread stopped being a hazard and became a targeted in-DRAM broadcast primitive.

codes: 07-17 session · docs/LATTICE_ADDRESSING_2026_07.md · feeds the M3 line

Safe-load reversed a block

Tried RowClone into tuples with the pair offset chosen by the lattice rules.
Result Corruption-free by construction, 16/16 — June's "clone corrupts tuples" block was a wrong model.
Pointed to The MVDRAM fast path un-blocked the same day: their computation-rows dataflow ran at 99.98% with safe placement, 2.2–2.3× per gate at ~0.17% end-to-end cost. June's "impossible" verdict reversed.

codes: T2 (2.2–2.3×/gate) · Result B reversal · Lane 2 · PR #1

The "probabilistic XDMA stall" lesson

Tried Living with weeks of intermittent DMA stalls blamed on the driver.
Result Root cause: program finalization ran on every execution, silently corrupting any program object executed more than once. One flag fixed it; 100-iteration re-execution now runs clean.
Why The finalizer re-inserted bookkeeping packets and OR-ed branch targets over baked bits — growth and garbage on re-run.
Pointed to Upstream DRAM-Bender carries the same latent bug (outreach item). Also: "probabilistic" is usually a deterministic bug seen from too far away.

codes: api patch 0001 (finalize-once) · upstream-relevant

Benders 1/3 rescue attempt negative, definitive

Tried Rescue the two Micron modules with lattice-aware screening.
Result Definitively MAJ3-limited: zero separated-generator lattices exist there.
Why Their decoder topology never fires the row combinations MAJ3 needs — structure, not luck.
Pointed to Storage/shuttle roles for those modules; compute stays on the hynix pair.

codes: S1 (selection-law fingerprints on benders 1/3) · memory dimm13_rescue

The merge: in-DRAM accumulation win

Tried Popcount accumulation inside DRAM (a carry-save tree of dual-track adders) with lattice-aware allocation.
Result 98.6–99.7% lane-exact and a 213× readout reduction at BitNet scale.
Pointed to The readout wall has an in-DRAM answer; the two roads began to merge.

Act IV July 20 – 21 · the throughput ladder and the wall model

Bonsai-1.7B on silicon win

Tried A second model family (PrismML Bonsai, Qwen3-class, g128 quantization).
Result Token-exact on the production die; then dual-DIMM 1.95×, fused operand loading 1.52×, and a 1-bit single-track protocol at 18.7 s/token — 5.36× stacked, and 1-bit now beats ternary.
Pointed to Generality beyond BitNet, and a ladder of levers worth naming individually.

codes: Lane 1 · V2S/V2G/V2GS · PR #2 · the 07-21 ladder (5.36×)

Request-path profiling hypothesis killed

Tried Instrumenting the server, suspecting the Python client's request assembly was half the wall.
Result Wrong: the client keeps the pipe full (~0.2 ms gaps). The handler is ~3 ms ≈ one program round-trip, and receive time is dominated by a fixed ~1.5 ms DMA wake, nearly independent of size.
Pointed to The real currency: host↔DRAM round-trips. Batching and packing became the levers — and they delivered the only real wall moves of the week (−12.7% stacked).

codes: lever 3b (original) · req-prof/srv-prof in the dispatcher

SEG_POP readback (bitstream build-7) wall-neutral

Tried Cut readback bytes 4× by returning per-segment popcounts instead of raw rows.
Result Silicon-exact, production token-identical — and the wall didn't move.
Why Receive time is latency-bound, not byte-bound; fewer bytes per wake changes nothing.
Pointed to Byte savings are banked, to be cashed once round-trips amortize. First confirmation of the cost model.

codes: lever A.1 · build-7 / magic 05 · PR #5 · PIM_SEGPOP

Driver poll mode negative

Tried Kill the DMA interrupt-wake tax with the driver's poll mode.
Result The read path returned I/O errors on the validation ladder; rolled back immediately, everything re-verified.
Pointed to The wake tax stands; the structural attack on it is streaming (Rung-1), not driver flags.

codes: lever 3a4 · fpga-helper load-poll (rolled back)

Bank scale-out win wall-neutral

Tried Run virgin banks with a calibration transferred verbatim from bank 0.
Result Token-identical on never-calibrated banks — transfer is "copy the line, byte-verify once". Sixteen banks later measured deposit-set md5-identical. Wall: neutral.
Why The physics is die-wide; but more residency doesn't cut round-trips, and receive time is size-sublinear.
Pointed to "Calibrate one bank, run the die" (a repo doc), and residency demoted as a wall lever.

codes: levers #11–#14 · O9 extras pilot · PR #7 · docs/CALIBRATION_TRANSFER.md

MAJ5 under a new reference policy honest negative

Result Chain-level accuracy 99.51% vs the MAJ3 route's 99.90% — the column gains don't survive composition.
Pointed to The SiMRA-style MAJ3 route stands; MAJ5 stays a science lane, not the production lever.

Act V July 22 · the inversion

ACCUM_XBP accumulator (bitstream build-8) net-negative in production

Tried An in-fabric accumulator that weights and sums bit-planes, draining one vector per group instead of one per plane.
Result An RTL saga (first image had a real alignment bug, reproduced in simulation, fixed, silicon-exact on both dies) — and then the production A/B came out +8% wall. Drains collapsed 3.6×; the wall went the wrong way.
Why The lever collapses drains, but the binding term is per-execute round-trips, which still stand — and the no-output executes paid a receiver tax on top.
Pointed to Second confirmation of the cost model. RTL effort redirected to the term that binds: streaming.

codes: lever A.0 · build-8a/8b / magic 06/07 · PR #9 · PIM_ACCUM_XBP

Dual-subarray residency wiring lesson

Tried The ledger said: wire the second subarray's pool into weight loading.
Result Code audit first: that "dual" path is dead code — zero call sites. The live mechanism (per-calibration extras + overflow) already does the job, and residency growth measures wall-neutral anyway.
Pointed to No code written — correctly. Read the code before wiring the ledger's premise.

M3 coset-deposit scratch loading, gates 1 & 2 win discovery

Tried Replace the per-column weight write (≈4,500 instructions, 3 programs) with one lattice deposit from a resident row.
Result Byte-exact on production pools, both dies, at 17 instructions in 1 program — 265.8× fewer instructions, ~4× wall. Then gate 2: 800-class shadow-pair supply per bank, every offset class byte-exact (including bit-9 where the window allows it), deposits chain (a deposit target is a valid clone source), fan-out to 7 rows per operation is clean, and 32 loads packed into one program ran 114× faster than the write path.
Why The first tool run also failed beautifully: the production pool contains no coupled pairs at all — it was screened to make writes safe, which excludes exactly the rows deposits need. The coupling the old layout avoided is the load channel.
Pointed to A shadow-row allocator (pair each resident row with its coupled shadow), sources enrolled in the existing refresh cadence — and the burst number means this pays before streaming lands, then becomes the top lever after.

Rung-1 streaming fetch (bitstream build-9) in flight two lessons

Tried A ping-pong instruction memory: load program N+1 while N executes, so the DRAM command bus stops waiting on the host between programs.
Result The simulation gate caught a real silicon hazard before any flash (the bank swap skipped the new program's first word — a one-cycle read-latency seam). The host streaming side is written and unit-proven. The first flashed image reported the old version magic: one file (the version-stamped readback engine) was never pushed to the build box — my staging omission. The ladder still passed on it, which silently validated the risky half (the new frontend). Rebuild is running.
Pointed to Why gates exist, twice over: the simulation catch, and the magic check refusing an image that can't identify itself. Pre-launch checklist now includes md5-ing every staged file and grepping the source set for the new magic.

Act VI July 23 · what the noise turned out to be

The streaming "corruption" that wasn't discovery methodology

Tried The producer loop's first full-model run diverged. A per-op dump showed the streamed answer differing from the legacy one on a specific, structured set of elements — only the odd-indexed ones, every operation, phase-locked. It looked exactly like a digital bug in the readout packer.
Result A day of chasing plausible mechanisms — byte lanes, packing counters, fetch hazards — each refuted. Then the cheap control nobody had run: compare two legacy runs to each other. They differed identically — same odd-only structure, same magnitude. The effect had nothing to do with streaming. Any two separate server processes disagree on ~all odd 32-bit segments (the upper half of every 64-bit transfer — chips 4–7 of the module), plus the same fixed 13 even ones, deterministically within a boot.
Why Unmodeled structural determinism masquerades as noise. The comparison itself was invalid: cross-process results were never comparable, because a per-boot analog operating point (the chip's init/training state) sets a slightly different margin for the upper DQ half. We had been reading a boot-to-boot silicon fingerprint and calling it a datapath fault.
Pointed to A new instrument and a new rule. The instrument: a captured production request stream replayed twice inside one process, legacy-vs-streamed back-to-back — the only comparison below the cross-process floor. The rule, now standing: gate ≈ control, and design the control first. It converted a phantom bug into a determinism map (screened kernels bit-exact; resident matmuls bit-exact but for refresh-straddle steps; raw un-screened requests ~95%-element metastable jitter that the vote/margin stack silently absorbs; the per-boot shift on top).

codes: the inversion · PIM_REQ_CAPTURE + replay --dup · memory cross_process_floor · PR #12

The producer loop, finally measured win

Tried With streaming exonerated, the same-process gate could actually bless it: replay twins, gate ≈ control to within one element in 345 requests.
Result Streaming on: −4% wall, token-identical, shipped default-on. Then the send-ahead pipeline (host builds program N+1 while N's result drains): −29% measured on the full model (1,307 s vs 1,848 s). A resident-constants lever, resurrected because streaming changed its economics, added −31% on its own — they multiply toward roughly 2× on the request wall.
Why Streaming already hid the per-program round-trip, so readout time became silicon-execution time — which flipped the whole cost model. Program count stopped being the wall (a K-batching lever, tried honestly, came back +19%); the un-overlapped host build/send became the target the pipeline attacks.
Pointed to The controller-native execution regime MVDRAM describes — the command bus as the clock — now built on our card. The remaining request-path levers are constant factors; the class-changer (in-DRAM accumulation) is the next arc.

codes: Rung 1 → phase-2 · PIM_STREAM / PIM_STREAM_PIPE · PR #14

Act VII July 24 · the fetch saga

A one-in-4,000 stall that was deterministic all along discovery simulation

Tried The pipeline ran clean for thousands of requests, then wedged. It smelled like a rare race — but "rare stochastic" and "deterministic with a deferred trigger" look identical in one sample.
Result A rerun sailed past the first stall point: position not fixed, so environmental arrival, not a fixed counter. The simulation testbench — extended with randomized program lengths and execution times instead of the fixed values it had always used — reproduced the loss instantly, 3 of 3 seeds, and self-shrank it to a four-program script. The fetch stage re-loops pc=0…END between a program's end and its completion signal; any hold parks the counter at an arbitrary phase, and the next program resumes from it, losing its first words. Latent in the legacy path too — masked for the project's whole life because every testbench pinned the timing that happened to land the freeze on zero.
Why Every parameter a testbench fixes is an assumption in disguise, and this bug lived exactly in the pinned dimension. The happy path was verified faithfully; the failure was never aimed at.
Pointed to Randomized stress with a checker as a permanent pre-flash gate. The fix (build-11): a program-start strobe forcing the counter to zero. Regression across every scenario plus 5×4,000 randomized programs: clean.

codes: build-11 / magic 0A · scenario F (self-shrinking) · fetch_restart · PR #14

The fix that broke the thing the sim couldn't see the model's edge

Tried Flash build-11. The streaming ladder passed clean — the stall was cured on silicon.
Result But one arm regressed: streamed branch-loop writes — the exact shape production's fused bodies use — now corrupted, where they had been fine before. The restart strobe un-parks the previous program's re-loop from where a branch used to harmlessly strand it, and that stale state becomes execute-visible. A branch-capable testbench (branches had been tied off) exercised the shape — but still couldn't reproduce the exact failure, because it happens where fetch crosses into the decode/execute side the sim stubs.
Why An ALL_PASS from a simulation is a statement about the verification boundary, not the design. This fix changed an invariant whose consumers live across that boundary; the moment a fix does that, the sim goes structurally silent on it. It is the same edge the end-to-end memory model has: the model omits exactly the part you're overlooking.
Pointed to Build-12: rather than patch symptoms, remove the disease. Fetch parks at end-of-program until the restart strobe — no re-loop, so the whole stale-state hazard class (stale counter, stale branch, post-end junk) cannot exist. Streaming reverted default-off on build-11 meanwhile; legacy paths stay exact, so production is safe at the −4% baseline until build-12's silicon gate.

codes: build-12 / magic 0B · park-at-END (WAIT_RESTART) + branch fence · scenario I · PR #14

The through-line

The cost model, earned the hard way — then inverted, then re-earned. Three "obvious" walls were attacked and each came back neutral or worse — readback bytes (SEG_POP), drain wakes (ACCUM_XBP), instruction count (M3's first gate moved the wall only 4× despite cutting instructions 265×). The one quantity whose reduction always moved the wall was the host↔DRAM round-trip count — until streaming destroyed it, and the regime inverted exactly as predicted: readout became silicon time, program count stopped mattering (K-batching, tried, +19%), and the un-overlapped host path became the target the pipeline cut by 29%. Every neutral and negative verdict was load-bearing. And the deepest lesson wasn't about the wall at all: the two hardest bugs of the month were a structural determinism dressed as noise, and a years-latent fault dressed as a rare race — both invisible until a control run and a randomized testbench forced them into the light. The house rule that nothing is "noise" until an experiment proves randomness paid for itself twice in two days.

Where this points now

  • Build-12 → flash → the branch-loop gate → streaming back on → the pipeline and constants walls land the measured ~2× on the request wall.
  • Then the merge — in-DRAM accumulation, the one lever that changes the complexity class of the wall (what crosses the bus goes from row-count to output-count — a measured 213× on the readout term) rather than a constant factor. Its prerequisites — streaming, the M3 deposit machinery, the shadow allocator — all matured as side effects of the work above.
  • Alongside, rolling: the science this campaign produced as exhaust — the selection law, the deposit direction, the determinism map, the complementarity with the field's own PuDGhost characterization — assembled and published as it settles, not held to the end.
